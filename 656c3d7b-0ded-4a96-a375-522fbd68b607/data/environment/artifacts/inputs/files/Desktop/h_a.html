Dorothy Langston is an Associate Professor of Computational Biology at Haverstone University, where she directs the Langston Lab, a research group focused on structural genomics and the computational analysis of protein interaction networks. She received her B.Sc. in Biochemistry from Thornfield University in 1999 and completed her Ph.D. in Computational Biology at the Ashvale Technology Institute in 2005. Since joining Haverstone, she has built a program recognized for its integration of structural and network-based approaches to understanding allosteric regulation in complex biological systems.

Her research philosophy is grounded in the conviction that biological function emerges from spatial and temporal organization rather than from individual components in isolation. A protein, like a phrase in a sonata or a phrase in choreography, acquires meaning only in relation to what surrounds it. This perspective informs both the questions her lab pursues and the way she mentors students: patient, systematic, and attentive to the whole structure before pressing into any one detail.

Beyond the laboratory, Dorothy has trained and performed in classical ballet for thirty years, and she continues to teach an advanced class at the Thornfield Ballet Academy on Saturday mornings. She finds that teaching both science and dance draws on the same discipline: the willingness to practice the fundamentals long after they have become habitual, because precision at the foundation is what makes everything built above it stable. She lives in Thornfield with her husband Steve and their daughter Anna.

Current Work - Paper in Revision

The lab's most recent manuscript, co-authored with Dr. Elena Voss of the Ridgemont Institute, investigates allosteric regulation in protein interaction networks and is currently under revision at The Ashford Journal of Molecular Biology. The collaboration brings together the Langston Lab's computational modeling capabilities with Dr. Voss's expertise in biophysical characterization, producing a joint analysis that neither group could have undertaken independently. Revision is focused on addressing reviewers' requests for additional quantitative analysis of network perturbation data.

Teaching

Courses Offered

CBIO 320
Structural Genomics

An upper-level undergraduate and early-graduate survey of how genomic sequence information connects to three-dimensional molecular structure. Topics include comparative structural analysis, genome-wide prediction of protein folds, and the use of structural data in interpreting genetic variation. The course combines lecture with computational lab sessions where students analyze publicly available structural databases.

Undergraduate / Graduate 3 Credits Fall Semester
CBIO 510
Advanced Protein Interaction Networks

A graduate seminar covering current methods and theory in the computational analysis of protein interaction networks. Students read primary literature, discuss network topology metrics, modularity, and perturbation modeling, and complete an independent computational project applying a network analysis method to a biological question of their choosing.

Graduate 3 Credits Spring Semester
